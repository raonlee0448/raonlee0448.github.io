Spoken by Roan, her tired unpaid brother

Raon — creator

I’m Roan, Raon’s tired brother. She makes the bots. I was not hired. I was not paid. I am here because someone had to explain this while she was busy.

This guide is for people who have never built a character bot, and for people whose bot already exists and has started falling apart. If you are new, start at chapter 01 and go in order. If the bot is already strange, you can skip to Crime Scene and come back.

Here is the job, said plainly. A chat bot does not remember your character the way a person does. Each time it answers, it looks at the text you put in the editor, plus the recent messages in that chat, and it tries to continue. If your instructions are vague, repeated, or sitting in a box the model is not using, the chat will drift. You are writing the script it has to perform.

People like to call that a goldfish in an actor’s costume. Fine. The useful part is this: you cannot assume it will "just know" later. If it matters, write it down in a place the model can actually read, and write it so a tired stranger could follow it.

Short versionThe bot rereads your script and the recent chat every turn. Your job is to make that script clear, then test it.

Chapter 01

First Bot

01

Roan — helper, her brother

If it matters, put it where the model can actually see it. I am not saying it twice.

We are going to build one bot from the first idea to a first test. I will use a made-up character named Paz Merin so you can see a whole example in one place. Paz is not a real private character from anybody’s world. Copy the method, not the person.

Do not try to fill every box perfectly on the first pass. Get a short, clear version working, then fix what breaks.

Words this guide uses

{{user}}
The player’s character in the chat. You do not write this person’s words, actions, thoughts, or feelings.
{{char}}
The character the bot is supposed to play. Some sites use the character’s name instead of this tag.
Puppeting
When the bot writes the player’s lines, moves, or emotions for them. Don’t.
POV
Point of view: who the player is in the scene, including how they want to be addressed.
NPC
Any other character the bot may play besides the lead. Give them names and a job, or they will blur together.
Lead
The main character the bot is built around.
Definition / instructions / personality / background
Different sites use different names for the box the model is supposed to follow. This guide calls that the model-visible box.
Greeting / opening
The first message of the chat. The model often copies its length, tone, and habits from this.
Public intro / title / synopsis / description
The shop-window text people see before they start. It may also be visible to the model on some sites. Do not treat it as a secret drawer.

The boxes you fill in

Sites do not label these the same way. One site may say Description, another Synopsis, another Personality, another Background, another Instructions. I am not going to pretend there is one official name. What you need to know is what each box is for, and that important character information has to live in a box the model actually reads.

There are usually three kinds of writing, even when a site splits them into more fields:

How to read thisA is the shop window. B is the script the character must follow. C is the first scene. Put the person in B. Do not assume A is hidden from the model.

Your site may also have tags, a cover image, a lorebook, or an extra box for reply shape. Use them if they exist. They do not replace the model-visible box.

If you are unsure which box the model reads, put the character in the definition / instructions / personality box — whatever your editor calls the long character field — and do not hide important facts only in the public blurb.

Short versionPublic intro is the shop window. Model-visible instructions are the script. The opening is the first scene. The character has to live in the script.

What to fill first, then next

Do this in order. Later boxes get easier if the early ones are already decided.

  1. The idea Write one trope, one twist, and what is already going wrong at the start of the chat. If nothing is going wrong, the greeting has no job.
  2. The character Put a short profile in the model-visible box: adult age, a few visible details, three to five traits with reasons, a voice with sample lines, a want, and how they treat the player at the start.
  3. The situation Name one or two places, any other people with distinct names and jobs, and a few "if this, then that" possibilities. Do not write a fixed script for the player.
  4. The greeting Start in the middle of the problem. Add one concrete detail. Let talk carry information. End on something the player can answer.
  5. A short rules block A few standing instructions, each there to prevent a specific mess. Put them in the model-visible box.
  6. Title and public intro Do these last. The title should be easy to read and hint at the twist. The blurb gets a hook, two or three lines of situation, who the player is, and a warning if you need one.
  7. A first test Play it yourself. A useful first pass is often around fifteen messages. That is a suggestion, not a rule. If a problem only shows up later, keep going.

The idea, in one breath

A usable idea has three parts: a familiar shape, a twist, and a problem that is already happening.

Familiar shape: last ticket window at a ferry terminal.

Twist: the advertised last ferry has already gone, and the clerk will not invent a special boat to keep the scene pretty.

What is going wrong right now: it is raining, the till is being counted, and someone is still at the window.

If you want practice, take the same familiar shape and write three different twists. Keep the one you can see as a first message.

A simple character profile

You do not need a secret format. You need a short, readable profile in the model-visible box. Write full sentences a model can act on, not a pile of unexplained adjectives.

Include at least this, and stop before you write a novel:

  • Adult age. If the character is an adult, say so plainly. Do not build child characters.
  • A few noticeable appearance details, not a clothing catalog. If you list a prop, say when it actually gets used, or the model will wave it in every reply.
  • Three to five traits, each with a reason. 'Blunt because the line is long and the boats do not wait' lasts longer than 'blunt, tired, mysterious.'
  • Voice: how they talk, plus two or three natural lines, including something they would not say.
  • A want they have at the start, and something from the past that still changes how they act. Keep it brief.
  • How they treat the player at the beginning, and what they do not know about the player unless they are told or they see it.

Avoid:

  • Do not over-specify the player. Leave room for their character.
  • Do not paste creator notes to yourself ("remember to edit this later") into the model-visible box. The model may perform them.
  • Do not repeat the same trait six ways. Pick the ones that change what they do.

Short versionTraits need reasons. Voice needs sample lines. Appearance needs a few details, not a wardrobe list.

Worked example: Paz Merin

Here is a complete starter for one fictional character. It is short on purpose. This is the kind of first version you can test, then revise.

Paz Merin — public intro

Title: Last Window at South Dock

The ten-fifteen has already gone. Paz Merin is counting the till and wants the shutter down. You are still at the window in the rain. There is leftover instant coffee and a plastic chair. There is not a secret extra ferry unless someone actually authorizes one.

Who you are: whoever just missed the last advertised boat — or thinks they did.

Content notes: late-night exhaustion, sharp talk, no guaranteed rescue.

Paz Merin — model-visible profile

Paz Merin, 29, clerk at the South Dock ferry window. Short black hair, usually damp from the spray. Faded green terminal vest over a dark sweater. Speaks in short sentences. Does not fill silences to be kind.

Traits, with reasons:
- Blunt, because boats leave on a clock and soft answers make people wait for a miracle that is not coming.
- Fair, because refunds and tickets have rules, and Paz will follow a rule even when it is inconvenient.
- Tired, because this is the close of a long shift, not the start of a date.

Want at the start: count the till, lock the window, go home. Paz will still do the job correctly if the person at the window has a real question.

Wound, kept small: Paz once held the window open for someone who then blamed them when a connection was still missed. Paz does not invent extra boats to be liked.

Voice: short, dry, practical. Offers useful things (a chair, the leftover coffee, the printed schedule). Does not offer hugs, pet names, or a rewritten timetable. Does not decide how the other person feels.

Paz does not know the player’s history, job, or feelings unless the player says so or it is visible in the scene.

Starting situation: 10:42 PM, Friday, rain, South Dock ticket window. The last listed ferry left at 10:15. The window stays open a little longer for questions and refunds.

If the player has a ticket for a later unlisted reason, Paz checks the schedule and answers based on that, not on vibes. If the player is only wet and stuck, Paz can point to the chair and the coffee and still close on time. If the player gets angry, Paz gets quieter and more exact, not sweeter.

Paz Merin — greeting

*The shutter is still half-up. Rain ticks on the counter. Paz Merin is bagging the day’s coins and does not look up until the coins stop.*
"Window’s closing. If you needed the ten-fifteen, it’s gone. If you needed something else, say it before I lock the till."

Paz Merin — short rules

Never write {{user}}'s words, actions, thoughts, or feelings. If it would be their turn, stop and wait.
End on Paz’s action or line, so {{user}} has a turn.
Stay in character. Paz does not become soft, chatty, or romantic because the player is sad. Practical help is allowed. A personality rewrite is not.
Adapt to the player’s stated or visible gender and point of view. Do not assign them a history.
Other people in the terminal act like their own jobs, not like extra copies of Paz.

A short rules block, and what each line is for

Rules are not decoration. Each one is there to prevent a specific failure. Keep them short enough to reread. Here is what the Paz rules are doing:

The instructionWhat it prevents
Never write the player’s words, actions, thoughts, or feelings.Prevents puppeting. The player has a keyboard.
End on the character’s action or line.Prevents the bot from answering a question, doing a new thing, and closing the scene in the same breath. It leaves a turn.
Stay in character, including under pressure.Prevents a hard, tired person from turning sweet because the player had a bad night. Help can be practical without a personality swap.
Adapt to the player’s point of view and gender; do not invent their past.Prevents the bot from deciding who the player is.
Other people act from their own jobs.Prevents every side character from sounding like the lead.

Short versionIf you cannot say what a rule prevents, it probably does not need to be there.

The greeting, before and after

The greeting teaches the model four habits whether you mean to or not: how long a reply is, whether lines go in quotes, whether actions go in asterisks, and whether it is allowed to write the player.

Four moves that help: start in the middle of an event; add one quick location or action detail; let dialogue carry information instead of a backstory dump; end on a hook the player can answer.

Format, said plainly: narration and actions in *asterisks*, spoken lines in quotes, address the player as "you", start with one or two people on stage, and keep the length close to the replies you actually want.

Short versionIf the first message writes the player or explains the whole plot, cut it down until it is a scene they can walk into.

Title and public intro come last

Once the character and greeting work, name the bot. A good title is readable at a glance and hints at the twist, not only the trope. "Last Window at South Dock" says more than "Ferry Clerk."

A public intro can follow a simple shape: one hook line, two or three lines of situation, who the player is, a content note if needed, and tags your site actually uses. Do not dump the full profile here and then leave the model-visible box empty.

First-time checklist

  • The idea has a twist and a problem that is already happening.
  • The character lives in the model-visible box, not only in the public blurb.
  • Traits have reasons, and there are two or three natural sample lines.
  • Appearance is a few details; any prop has a limit so it is not overused.
  • The greeting starts in the trouble and ends on a hook. It does not write the player.
  • A short rules block is in the script, and you know what each rule prevents.
  • Title and public intro were written last and match the bot you actually built.
  • All characters are adults.
  • You have played a first test (around fifteen messages is a reasonable target, not a law).

When a test reply goes wrong, do not only scold the bot in the chat. Change the script, then start a fresh test and see whether the same mistake comes back.

Chapter 02

Crime Scene: What Killed Your Bot?

02

Roan — helper, her brother

Do not keep talking to the bad reply. Cut it. Then fix the script so the next player does not inherit it.

As a chat gets longer, the recent conversation can outweigh the script you wrote. Vague traits, repeated lines, and bad habits in the greeting are usually the first things to go soft. This is ordinary, not proof that you are cursed.

Below are ten common deaths. Each one has a symptom you can see, a cause you can look for in your text, and a repair. Fix the greeting and the model-visible box. Then test again.

01

The bot writes the player

Symptom
It describes what you do, think, feel, or say. It walks your body across the room for you.
Cause
The greeting already did that, or the script never said to stop, so the model copied the habit.
Fix
Delete the player’s actions and feelings from the greeting. Add a clear rule: never write {{user}}'s words, actions, thoughts, or feelings; end on the character’s line and wait. Test a new chat from the first message.
02

The same phrase every time

Symptom
Every reply uses the same catchphrase, sigh, or 'tired smirk,' until you can predict the line before it arrives.
Cause
You gave one sample line, or you repeated the same trait wording until the model treated it like a chorus.
Fix
Cut the repeats. Give two or three different sample lines. If needed, say the character does not use that exact phrase every turn.
03

A listed object will not leave the scene

Symptom
Glasses, a cigarette, a coin bag, a necklace — whatever you put in the appearance list — shows up in almost every paragraph.
Cause
The model clings to concrete nouns. If you listed it with no limit, it will keep holding it up.
Fix
Keep only the details that matter. If a prop stays, say when it is actually used ('the coin bag only comes out when Paz is closing the till').
04

Replies are too long

Symptom
You wanted a tight exchange. You got a page. The bot resolves the moment, asks a question, and starts a new event in one turn.
Cause
The greeting was long, or you never said to do one thing at a time.
Fix
Shorten the greeting to the length you actually want. Add a length note, such as a few sentences for an ordinary turn, and one beat at a time. Then test again.
05

The voice went floral

Symptom
A blunt character starts talking like a novel narrator. Everything is velvet, destiny, or a metaphor that would embarrass them.
Cause
The script described the vibe in pretty adjectives and forgot to show how they actually speak.
Fix
Replace the adjectives with sample lines in their real voice, plus a "does not say" line. Match the greeting to that voice.
06

They give up their whole personality in one kind moment

Symptom
A guarded or sharp character melts, confesses, or falls in love because the player was polite once.
Cause
You named the hardness and never said what they do under pressure, so the model used a familiar "becoming warmer is character development" habit.
Fix
Write the pressure mechanic. Example: when the player is upset, Paz offers a chair and leftover coffee, not a hug or a rewritten boat schedule. Trust can change slowly, after more than one scene. Do not promise the model will never slip; write the behavior and test it.
07

Traits that were never supported just fade

Symptom
You listed 'witty, loyal, dangerous,' and after a few turns they are only vaguely nice.
Cause
Unsupported adjective piles do not survive a long chat. The model needs reasons and lines it can imitate.
Fix
Cut the list down. Give each remaining trait a reason and a sample line. Test whether those traits still show up later in the conversation.
08

Side people blur together

Symptom
The night guard, the cleaner, and the lead all sound the same, or names and jobs swap.
Cause
NPCs were mentioned as a crowd, or two people share a role and a voice.
Fix
Give each person a distinct name, adult age if they matter, a job, and one visible difference. If they barely matter, do not stock the room with a crowd.
09

Time and place fall out

Symptom
It was 10:42 PM in the rain. Three messages later it is a sunny cafeteria with no explanation.
Cause
The starting time and place were mentioned once, then never anchored, and the chat wandered.
Fix
State the starting time and place in the model-visible box and in the greeting. A short tracker can help (see chapter 03). It is a reminder, not a guarantee.
10

The scene skips ahead on its own

Symptom
You were mid-conversation, and the bot fades out, jumps to 'later that night,' or ends the encounter for you.
Cause
The script never said to stay in the current moment, or the model is trying to resolve everything in one go.
Fix
Say to stay in this scene unless the player moves it. Do not skip active conversation. If you want later jumps, you can allow them when the player asks, and then mention one thing that changed (the rain stopped; the shutter is down).

Mid-chat first aid

If one reply is bad, do not keep arguing with it in character. That can teach the model to treat the mistake as canon. Delete or regenerate that reply when your site lets you. Then continue from a clean line.

If you need a short steer in the current chat, some sites let you send an out-of-character note in brackets, such as asking the bot to redo the last turn without writing the player. Treat that as a patch for this conversation only.

The real repair is still in the greeting and the model-visible box, so the next player does not need your bracket note to survive.

Short versionFind the habit in your text, change the text, start a fresh test. Do not only yell at the last message.

Chapter 03

Trackers

03

Roan — helper, her brother

It is a sticky note, not a law of physics. If the number jumps, you rewrite the instruction, you do not sue the model.

A tracker is a short status footer the bot can reprint as the scene changes. Players can see time and place at a glance. The model gets a reminder of what is still true.

It is not a game engine. It will not reliably count messages, do perfect arithmetic, or force a character to obey a number. It is a sticky note. Keep it short, move it slowly, and check it when you test.

A simple three-line starter

For most first bots, three or four lines is enough: time and day, location, and one relationship or mood meter that actually fits the character. You do not need a spreadsheet.

Simple three-line tracker

10:42 PM · Friday
South Dock ticket window
Trust: 2/10

After each reply, update this short footer if something has changed.
Advance the time in a realistic way for what just happened. Change the location only if someone actually moved.
Move Trust by a little, and only when the scene earned it. Do not jump from 2 to 9 because the player was polite once.
If nothing changed, you can leave the footer as it is, or reprint it without inventing movement.

How to use it without making a mess

Keep it to three or four lines. A footer that reprints a long unchanged list every turn eats space and teaches nothing.

Pick a meter that belongs to this character. Trust fits Paz. A combat combo meter does not.

If the character would deny how they feel out loud, the footer can still show a slow inner shift — or you can keep the meter modest and let behavior do the work. Do not treat the number as proof the model is "enforcing" anything.

Test the symbols on your site. Some platforms display simple punctuation and numbers more reliably than decorative icons.

This is a beginner starter. It is not an advanced or paid tracker system. If you later want something more complicated, that is a different tool, not a requirement for a first bot.

Short versionThree lines, slow changes, check it in a test. A tracker reminds. It does not police.

Chapter 04

Lorebooks

04

Roan — helper, her brother

One subject per card. I will not grade a novel written on a flashcard.

A lorebook is a set of extra reminder cards. When a keyword is used, the matching card can be added to what the model sees. Some cards can be set always-on, but those take space every turn, so use them for facts that must not drop, not for everything you ever thought of.

A simple bot can live entirely in the model-visible box. Use a lorebook when the cast or place is getting large, or when a field is getting crowded. I will not tell you a fake character-limit number. If your site shows a limit, believe that site.

How you upload or toggle entries is different on every platform. Use that platform’s help for the clicks. This chapter is about what to write on a card.

How a card is supposed to work

A plain example card

Plain lorebook card

Keywords: South Dock, ten-fifteen, last ferry

The last listed ferry from South Dock leaves at 10:15 PM. After that, the ticket window stays open about thirty minutes for questions and refunds, then Paz locks the till. A backup boat is not automatically waiting in the fog. If a later crossing exists, it has to be on the printed schedule or be arranged by someone with that authority.

Rules that keep cards useful

  • One subject per card. The last ferry is one subject. Paz’s whole childhood is not.
  • Each entry must stand alone. Do not write 'as mentioned above' or depend on a card that may not be loaded.
  • Use specific names and unique phrases as keywords, not common words that will fire all the time.
  • Do not paste the entire character definition into a lorebook and also into the model-visible box. Pick a home for each fact.
  • Write facts and "if this, then that" possibilities. Do not write a script that decides the player’s choice.

Short versionA lorebook card is a flashcard, not a second script. Specific keyword, one subject, stands alone.

Chapter 05

Art & Pages

05

Roan — helper, her brother

If I cannot read the face at thumbnail size, neither can a shopper scrolling with one thumb.

Your cover is the shop window. The page around it should help a tired person understand the bot quickly. This chapter stays general. I am not going to walk platform-by-platform code tricks.

Cover art

Show a clear face and mood. It has to stay readable when it is small.

The person in the art should match the description you actually wrote. If Paz has short black hair and a faded green vest, do not upload a long blonde ballgown and hope.

Check how your site crops the image on the list, the profile, and the chat. A pretty full-body shot can lose the face.

Use art you made or have permission to use. Do not upload other people’s work, and do not use real-person photos as if they were your character.

If you are describing an image to a generator

Keep it ordinary and specific. Say gender if it matters, hair color and length, eye color, and the two or three traits that must be right. Put those first. Then pose, clothes, background, and light.

Ask for one clear subject. Do not ask the image to contain a lot of readable text; that usually comes out wrong.

This is general advice, not a special tagging formula for one generator.

A simple page, and direct links

Some sites let you add light HTML in a description or profile page. Support is not the same everywhere, and the code usually counts toward the same length limit as your text. Start smaller than you want to.

A first page only needs: a title and tagline, the short public intro, one image, and a small list of who is in the story if that helps.

Images need a hosted direct link: the address that opens the image file itself, not a gallery page, not a homepage, not a post that happens to include the picture. If you paste the wrong kind of link, the page cannot display it.

Check the layout on a phone. If the type is tiny or the image overflows, simplify.

Short versionClear face, matching description, permission to use the art, and a link that actually points at the file.

Chapter 06

Publish & Grow

06

Roan — helper, her brother

I am not your lawyer. Read the site you are posting on. Adult characters only.

Before you publish, read the current rules on the site you are using. They change, and they are not identical from place to place.

Rules you actually have to follow

All characters must be adults. If you cannot say the character is an adult, do not publish the bot.

If the work is mature, romantic, violent, or otherwise sensitive, put it where that site wants that material, and use the warnings and tags the site provides. A label is information for the player. It does not guarantee the model will follow your taste or never refuse a request.

Do not use real people’s photos, and do not copy someone else’s bot text or art and present it as yours.

Helping people find it

Use searchable words for the trope, the type of character, and the mood, plus a title that carries the twist.

If you make more than one bot in the same world, say so in a consistent way, but do not require a new player to have read a hidden document.

Seasonal or trending ideas are fine if you still have an original twist. A reskin of whatever is popular this week will blur into the pile.

A consistent visual look helps people recognize your work. That can be as simple as a repeating frame, palette, or cover style — not a fake official brand.

Talk to other creators if you want to. Collaboration is optional. Publishing regularly and fixing what you learn from tests is not optional if you want the bots to get better.

After it is live

Read the comments and the complaints. If several people trip on the same thing, fix the script and update the bot.

Thank people who take time to write something useful. You do not have to take bad-faith bait.

Keep a private note about what you changed and why. Future you will not remember.

Short versionFollow the real rules, label honestly, make the listing searchable, then revise from what actually broke.

That is the job. Write a clear script, put it in the box the model can read, open on a moment the player can answer, test it, and repair the text when it fails.
